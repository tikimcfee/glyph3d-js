# Predictions: device-tier-scaling

## rendering-backend-portability

They likely concluded that the current hard coupling to Three.js and WebGL 2 is the central risk, and proposed an abstraction layer (a "GPU contract" or render-backend interface) that isolates the instanced-draw-call pattern behind a backend-agnostic API -- something like `submitInstances(attributeBuffers, uniforms, textures)` that could target WebGL 2, WebGPU, or hypothetically native APIs. They probably identified the GLSL shader strings inlined in GlyphRenderer.js and PickingSystem.js as the most problematic coupling point, since shader language differs across APIs (GLSL ES 3.00 vs WGSL), and proposed either a shader transpilation step or dual shader authoring. They almost certainly flagged the Three.js-specific abstractions (InstancedBufferGeometry, DataTexture, WebGLRenderTarget) as the seam where the abstraction layer would sit, and noted that WebGPU's compute-shader capabilities could replace some CPU-side buffer building.

## universal-text-pipeline

They likely analyzed the text-to-GPU-buffer pipeline (text -> glyph metrics -> layout -> Float32Array -> InstancedBufferAttribute) and concluded that the pipeline stages are already fairly well-separated thanks to the worker/builder architecture, but that the font/atlas stage is the hardest to make portable. The GlyphAtlas's dependency on Canvas 2D `measureText` and `drawText` for metrics and rasterization ties the entire system to browser APIs, so they probably proposed an abstract font-metrics interface that could be backed by Canvas 2D, HarfBuzz-wasm, or a pre-baked atlas asset. For the buffer contract, they likely defined a stage-boundary format: each pipeline stage consumes and produces typed arrays with documented layouts, making the stages independently replaceable. They may have flagged the per-glyph-width lookup (glyphWidths from atlas metrics) as the critical contract point between atlas and layout stages.

## data-source-abstraction

They likely concluded that the current system assumes files arrive as complete text strings (via `setText`/`addText` on CodeGrid), and proposed a provider interface that abstracts over data sources -- local filesystem, GitHub API, WebSocket relay, in-memory buffers. They probably identified incremental/streaming updates as the key missing capability: the current pattern of full-buffer rebuild on any text change is wasteful for large files with small edits, and they likely proposed a diff-based or range-update protocol where the provider can signal "lines 5-10 changed" and only those buffer regions get rebuilt. For lifecycle management, they probably noted that CodeGrid creation, content loading, and disposal are currently ad-hoc (scattered across IDEShell/GitHubRepoViewer), and proposed a lifecycle controller that manages grid creation, content binding, and memory reclamation -- tying into the GridVirtualizer's existing frustum-culling to trigger load/unload of content based on visibility.

## prior-art-lessons

They most likely studied Monaco Editor (VS Code's editor), xi-editor, Zed, and possibly Alacritty or wgpu-based terminal emulators as prior art for GPU-accelerated text rendering. From Monaco, they probably extracted the lesson that virtualization (only rendering visible lines) is non-negotiable for scale, and that the DOM fallback path is essential for accessibility. From xi-editor, they likely noted the CRDT/rope-based text model and the clean separation between frontend and backend as a pattern worth emulating, as well as the cautionary tale of its eventual abandonment due to complexity. From Zed and Alacritty, they probably identified the glyph-atlas-plus-instancing pattern as validated prior art, and noted that both projects solve the font-metrics problem via platform-native text shaping (CoreText, FreeType) rather than Canvas 2D. The key anti-pattern they likely flagged is "premature abstraction" -- trying to abstract too many backends before proving the core rendering path works well on even two targets.
